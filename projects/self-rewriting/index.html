At a glance

The paper in four results.

What if making a language model better no longer took another round of pre‑training or post‑training? A language model rewrites its own weights by its own reasoning and becomes better, with no gradients and no training.

Finding 01

It surpasses itself.

Its own edit makes it more accurate on problems it never saw.

 Finding 02

It keeps getting better.

Over eight consecutive sessions, its accuracy only rises.

 Finding 03

It repairs a damaged copy of itself.

No retraining, no access to the original weights: over 80% of its accuracy restored on image tasks.

 Finding 04

It emerges with scale.

Of four sizes (2B, 4B, 9B, 27B), only the largest improves and repairs itself.
  • Affordable.a few hours on one GPU and no added cost per answer
  • Scalable.it emerges with model size
  • Recursive.the model improves itself

It rewrites its own weights

It takes no frontier model

Earlier, a frontier model edited a much smaller one. Here an open 27B model rewrites itself.

It grows with scale

The first evidence that it scales: in one model family, only the largest improves itself.

The idea A third way

Until now, a language model got better in one of two ways. This is the third.

{% include selfrw/paradigm.html %}

The model is its own optimizer. It decides what to change, and why.

How it works

Real recursive self-improvement: the model that improves is the model improved.

From outside it learns only which practice answers were right. The diagnosis, the edit and the decision are its own.

  • no teacher
  • no judge
  • no reward model
  • no larger model
  • no harness engineering
  • no prompt engineering

The evidence Finding 02

It keeps getting better.

Over eight consecutive sessions, its accuracy only rises.

All eight sessions reported. Whiskers: 95% interval of the change.

It tests its edits on practice problems and submits only what works.

The evidence Finding 03

It repairs a damaged copy of itself.

Like a broken robot that fixes itself without being rebuilt: no retraining, and it never reads the original weights.

One block of 8 rows in one matrix is overwritten with noise, and the copy fails almost everything. After its own repair it is back from 7.3% to 70.2% on six image benchmarks: 83% of the undamaged model.

How it finds the damage

200 text problems it never saw. The values it writes are its own, not the originals.

Why it matters Finding 05

The gain lives in its weights.

Unlike test-time scaling, self-rewriting writes its gain into the weights, at no cost per answer; unlike training, it runs only forward passes, as serving does.

What this opens up

Learning without a pipeline

A signal, an edit, a check, a rollback: the whole loop, in one conversation.

Edits you can read

Every change is code the model wrote. Diff it, test it, revert it like a commit.

Open to everyone

Improving a model need no longer be a privilege of the few labs with the infrastructure and the GPUs.

The horizon

The wall between training and inference is a limit of our tools, not a property of intelligence.

We never stopped living in order to learn.

Trained once, then frozen

It fails the same way every day, until someone retrains it.

Learning while it works

It acts by day and rewrites its weights by night. What it could not do yesterday, it does today.

An illustration of what self-rewriting could make possible, not an experiment.

Learning by doing

Perhaps models can learn the way we do: by doing, by talking, by rewriting themselves.

Robots that keep learning

Perhaps a robot will act by day, and rewrite itself while it sleeps.

Learning for life

Perhaps we have knocked on the door of lifelong continual learning.

We believe self-rewriting is a necessary step toward recursive self-improvement: a model that, like a human, does not separate training from inference could keep upgrading itself for life, with no larger model, no harness or prompt engineering, and no pre- or post-training.
