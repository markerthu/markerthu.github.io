At a glance

The paper in four findings.

Best-of-N search for text-to-image models finishes every candidate before it judges it. Keystone judges candidates at the beginning, from the model’s own blurry previews, and spends compute only where the outcome is still open.

Finding 01

The outcome is decided in the first steps.

Change the noise of one of the first two steps and the verdict changes most often; by step 8 of 40 it can already be read.

 Finding 02

A blurry preview, read better than a finished image.

Asked the prompt’s own questions, an off-the-shelf decision model reads counts and positions from a step-8 preview better than reward models read them from the finished image.

 Finding 03

More accurate, at under half the compute.

Keystone’s searches beat best-of-8 with every other verifier while finishing far fewer images.

 Finding 04

A drop-in judge that travels.

It lifts best-of-N, progressive seed pruning and FK steering; without retuning, its own searches stay above every other verifier’s best-of-N, on average, across four base models and three benchmarks.
  • Training-free.a released judge; no step labels, no reward model trained on noise
  • Reads previews.the model’s own clean-image estimate, at no extra network evaluations
  • Plugs in.any diffusion or flow sampler, any search that keeps candidates

The problem

Best-of-N finishes every image before it judges it, even the ones doomed from the start.

Text-to-image models still miscount, drop and misplace objects. The standard remedy, best-of-N, draws several complete images and keeps the one a verifier prefers. The verifiers are reward models trained on finished images, so the generator pays in full for every candidate the search will throw away.

{% include proj/keystone/problem.html %}

The image was never the problem. The judge was.

The idea

The layout is in place long before the picture is: ask the preview the prompt’s own questions.

Every denoising step yields the model’s own estimate of the finished image. Decoded, it is a blurry preview, but how many objects there are, which ones and where is already visible.

{% include proj/keystone/idea.html %} {% include proj/keystone/compile.html %}

Keystone changes the judge instead of training one.

How it works

Compile the prompt, read at the key steps, search where it matters.

Three parts, and none of them is trained.

  • no training
  • no step labels
  • no gradient through the judge
  • no change to the sampler
{% include proj/keystone/pipeline.html %}

The previews’ answers decide which candidates receive further compute; nothing is differentiated through the judge and the sampler is unchanged.

The evidence Finding 01

Decided in the first steps, readable by step 8.

Change only the noise of one step and see how often the official verdict changes; then ask how well each judge orders candidates from the preview at each step.

{% include proj/keystone/f01.html %}

Search should branch at the start and read shortly after.

The evidence Finding 02

A step-8 preview, read better than a finished image.

On identical candidates, the Keystone score reads counts and positions from step-8 previews better than reward models read them from finished images.

{% include proj/keystone/f02.html %}

Reading the preview well is what lets search stop early.

The evidence Finding 03

More accurate than every other verifier’s best-of-8, at under half its compute.

On GenEval with SD3.5-Medium, both searches lie above best-of-N with every judge at the same cost.

{% include proj/keystone/f03.html %}

Pruning reaches 0.82 at 144 network evaluations; every other verifier’s best-of-8 costs 320 and stays at or below 0.78.

The evidence Finding 04

A drop-in judge, across models and benchmarks.

Swap the judge inside existing methods, or move to other models and benchmarks without retuning.

{% include proj/keystone/f04.html %}

Averaged over models, both searches beat the strongest other verifier’s best-of-N on all three benchmarks.

Why it matters

Test-time search no longer has to finish an image to judge it.
