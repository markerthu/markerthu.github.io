At a glance

The paper in four results.

Deep reinforcement learning agents tend to learn fast or end strong, rarely both. What if both come down to one thing: which data the agent learns from? GDI learns which behaviors should collect its data, and sets a new state of the art on Atari with a fraction of the data.

Finding 01

It sets a new state of the art.

The highest mean human-normalized score on 57 Atari games, ahead of Agent57 and MuZero.

 Finding 02

It needs 500 times less data.

Agent57-level play on a five-hundredth of its experience, and 22 human world records.

 Finding 03

Choosing the data is the lever.

Take away the operator that steers the data, and the same agent falls far behind.

 Finding 04

Richer behaviors see more.

A larger space of behavior policies visits more of the game and scores higher.

The problem

Top agents end strong by trying to see everything, and pay for it in data.

Agent57 and Go-Explore try to visit every unseen situation. They ignore which data is worth having, so many of their trials collect data of little value.

When interactions are limited, which data you collect matters as much as how much.

The idea One lever

Learning fast and ending strong are one problem: the distribution of the training data.

Two challenges

Sample efficiencylearn from few interactions
Final performanceend as strong as possible

Two classic problems

Data richnesshow much of the game the data covers
Explore or exploitnew situations, or valuable ones

One problem

Optimize the training data distributionget the data it needs within limited interactions

Two controls

Capacity and diversityhow many behavior policies exist, and how many are used
Selective distributionhow often each one plays, adapted as it learns

Control which behavior policies collect the data, and you control both.

How it works

One loop with two updates: one for the policy, one for the data.

Generalized policy iteration updates the agent from its data. GDI adds a second update for which behavior policies collect that data. Switch it off, or shrink the space of behaviors, and familiar algorithms appear.

Seen through GDIseven algorithms, one loop

The same loop with different settings

Schematic of the paper’s operator view. Blue: the RL update of the learner. Green: the update of the data distribution, done by a meta-controller; dashed when an algorithm has none.

The loop stays the same. What changes is whether, and how, the data is chosen.

The evidence Finding 01

It sets a new state of the art on Atari.

Across the 57 games of the Arcade Learning Environment, GDI reaches the highest mean human-normalized score of every agent compared.

Mean human-normalized score (HNS) over 57 Atari games, from the paper’s full comparison table. Green: GDI. Right: frames each agent trained on.

Ending strong no longer requires collecting everything.

The evidence Finding 02

It needs 500 times less data than Agent57.

It beats Agent57’s mean score and breaks more human world records, on a five-hundredth of the experience.

Training frames, equivalent human playtime and human world records broken (out of 57 games), from the paper’s comparison tables.

Choosing the data turned decades of play into weeks.

The evidence Finding 03

Choosing the data is the lever.

Fix the behavior policy to a single point, so nothing steers the data, and the same agent falls far behind.

Same space of behaviors, same RL update, same hyperparameters. The only change: no meta-controller, so the behavior policy keeps one fixed index and the data distribution is never optimized.

The theory agreesWith the same RL update, updating the data distribution never lowers the target of the next update (Theorem 1), nor its expected improvement under natural gradients (Theorem 2), under the paper’s assumptions.

Normalized by GDI-I³ (dashed line at 1). Bars as drawn in the paper’s ablation figure, which prints no values.

Collecting data is not enough. Choosing it is what works.

The evidence Finding 04

A richer space of behaviors sees more of the game.

Shrink the behaviors to one temperature knob and the agent scores lower; build them from two separate networks and it scores higher.

Left: normalized by GDI-I³, as drawn in the paper’s ablation figure. Right: the paper’s t-SNE of 6k Seaquest states from three stages of training.

More kinds of behavior give richer data, and richer data gives a stronger agent.

Why it matters

The data an agent learns from is not a given. It is a lever.

GDI turns the choice of behavior policies into a learnable part of reinforcement learning, with a framework, a proof and a recipe.

One loop, many algorithms

DQN, R2D2, LASER, PBT, Agent57 and Go-Explore become special cases, which makes each easier to understand.

A guarantee, not a heuristic

With a few assumptions, optimizing the data distribution is proven never to hurt the RL update it feeds.

Superhuman on a small budget

All of it on one GPU and one CPU, from the equivalent of weeks of human play.
