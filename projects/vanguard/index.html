At a glance

The paper in four results.

What if the small model went first, and the large model wrote only the steps it gets wrong? With a judge that checks each drafted step against the screen, the small model’s drafts can be more accurate than the large model alone.

Finding 01

A small draft surpasses its large target.

On websites it has never seen, the large model writes only about a quarter of the steps, and the result is significantly more accurate than the large model alone.

 Finding 02

On the web, on phones and in homes.

As accurate as the large model on phones, more accurate in homes, and far more episodes solved in closed loop.

 Finding 03

Across model families: faster and more accurate.

A small Qwen draft with a large Gemma target is faster than the large model alone, and significantly more accurate on all three agent tasks.

 Finding 04

One rule says when it pays off.

The gain grows with the small model’s headroom: how often one of its four drafts is right, beyond how often the large model is right.
  • Training-free.only the question and one threshold are set, on development data
  • Across model families.no draft heads and no shared tokenizer, so the target can be another family
  • Sees the screen.each drafted step is judged with the screenshot in view

The problem

Step-level speculation rests on its judge, and it has been measured by how little it loses.

Agents that act from screenshots reason before every action, so every step waits for a large model. Step-level speculation lets a small model draft the step, and the large model writes only the steps a judge rejects.

The large model grades

Its probabilities sit almost all near 0 or 1, so a threshold on them accepts or rejects nearly everything.

A process reward model

It is specialised to one domain and must be trained for it.

The small model’s own signals

They inherit whatever it fails to notice about its own mistakes.

A judge that reads only text

A fluent step can click an element that is not on the screen, and a text-only judge cannot tell.

The judge decides what is saved and what is lost, and whether the small model can end up ahead.

The finding

Four drafts of a small model can hold a right step about as often as the large model is right.

Sample the 2B draft four times on the same step: one of its four answers is right about as often as the 8B gets the step right, and not always on the same steps.

Qwen3-VL 2B draft, four samples at temperature 0.7, against the Qwen3-VL 8B target decoding greedily. Mind2Web pools held-out tasks and unseen websites.

The raw material for beating the large model is already there. What is missing is a judge that can pick it out.

How it works

One question, asked with the screen in view.

For each drafted step, a general decision model is asked whether the step is right, given the goal, the screenshot and the previous actions. The best draft is kept if the answer is confident; otherwise the large model writes the step.

  • no training
  • no draft heads
  • no shared tokenizer
  • no generated text

On web and phone tasks it has to see the screen: with the screenshot removed, or swapped for another task’s, it falls to or below the large model.

The evidence Finding 03

Across model families: faster and more accurate.

Vanguard exchanges whole steps, not tokens, so the target can come from another family. With a Qwen3-VL 2B draft and a Gemma-3 27B target, it is faster than the 27B alone and significantly more accurate on all three agent tasks.

Step success (%) on the full test sets; all seven gains are significant. Speed: end to end at batch size 1 against the 27B alone, on the first 300, 200 and 300 test steps of Mind2Web, AndroidControl and EB-ALFRED.

No draft heads, no shared tokenizer: a small model of one family can lead a large model of another.

The evidence Finding 04

One rule says when it pays off.

A judge can only choose among the drafts, so the small model can surpass its target only where its four drafts hold a right step that the target misses. Across sixteen results in four model families, the gain grows with the headroom: how often one of the four drafts is right, minus how often the target is right.

Sixteen (draft, target, task) results across Qwen3-VL, Qwen2.5-VL, Qwen3.5 and Gemma-3, on Mind2Web, AndroidControl and EB-ALFRED. Filled: significant gain over the target. Dashed: least-squares line. Pearson r = 0.95.

Where the headroom is largest, Vanguard turns more than half of it into a significant gain.

Why it matters

A judge that can tell lets the large model write only the steps the small one gets wrong.

Vanguard is the most accurate judge compared on the agent tasks, tied only by Qwen3-VL-32B on Mind2Web, and its scores are calibrated, so one threshold carries over from development data.

Judge accuracy over three model families and three agent tasks. With one τ = 0.5, the 8B writes 23% of the development steps, 26% on held-out tasks and 23% on unseen websites.

What this opens up

Nothing to train

No draft heads, no shared tokenizer, no new reward model. The draft and the target stay as they are.

With token-level speculation

With EAGLE-3 inside both models, Vanguard comes close to EAGLE-3’s speed and, on unseen websites, is significantly more accurate than EAGLE-3 alone: 68.4% against 63.5%.

Beyond agents

On MathVista and ProcessBench it is on par with process reward models trained for these domains; picking one of four solutions on MATH-500, it significantly beats the math reward model.

Speculation was measured by how little it loses. Now the small model can lead.
